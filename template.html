Fragments Made Whole

Nine broken works, each restored twice by an image model: once knowing nothing, once knowing what the museum knows. The question is what the machine makes up.

A course project by ruminate18 · built with the Harvard Art Museums API and Prodia

The experiment

Same model, same photograph, two prompts. Every fragment's museum photograph went to the same image model (Gemini 3 Pro, through Prodia) twice.

Uninformed: {{UNINFORMED}}, the same words for every object.

Evidence-led: a prompt built only from the museum's record and published scholarship, which tells the model what survives, what is known and what not to add.

Then each result was read against the record: what the evidence supports, and what the model invented. {{N_IMAGES}} images, {{SPENT}} of course credit.

Almost everything in an archaeology gallery is a piece of something larger. A head without its body, a band without its tunic, a painting cut out of its book. Museums answer the question "what was the rest?" carefully, because the answer becomes what visitors believe. The 1964 Venice Charter, still the reference point for conservators, says restoration "must stop at the point where conjecture begins." Cesare Brandi's theory of restoration asked that any new work be recognizable as new when you look closely, so no one mistakes a repair for the original.

Generative image models don't stop where conjecture begins. Ask one to restore a fragment and it answers at once, in seamless high resolution, with no visible join between what survives and what it imagined. That is the Ecce Homo problem at machine speed: in 2012 an amateur's well-meant repainting of a fresco in Borja, Spain, replaced a damaged face with a new one. The difference is that the model's version looks right.

The models also carry assumptions. Ancient sculpture and relief were painted, but centuries of white marble in museums trained the modern eye, and probably the training data, to expect bare stone. Reconstructions like Vinzenz Brinkmann's Gods in Color still surprise visitors. So the experiment asks two things of each fragment. What does a model assume when it knows nothing? And when the prompt carries the museum's evidence (pigment traces, the slab next door in another museum, the tunic in Boston), does the guess get better, or only more confident?

The nine works come from the Harvard Art Museums and were chosen for the kind of loss they show: lost color, lost body, lost room, lost garment, lost floor, lost page, and in the last case, almost everything. Each case below shows the fragment, both reconstructions, the evidence, and a reading of what the evidence supports and what the model invented.

How it was made

  • Images generated with Gemini 3 Pro (image-to-image) through the course's Prodia proxy, one call per image, no retries chosen for looks: every result is shown.
  • Source code, prompts, cost log and method: github.com/ruminate18/fragments-made-whole.
  • AI reconstructions are speculative images, not evidence. Do not reuse them as depictions of the originals.
